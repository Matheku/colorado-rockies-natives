01 — The gradient

Six thousand feet,
three worlds.

Every thousand feet you climb in Colorado is worth roughly three hundred miles of travel north. Temperature falls, the growing season shortens, and the wind stops being weather and starts being a design constraint. Species do not spread evenly up the slope. They stack.

  1. Above 11,500 ft

    Alpine

    No trees. Growing season under ten weeks. Cushion plants here can take a decade to spread an inch, and many of them also grow in the Arctic.

  2. 9,500 – 11,500 ft

    Subalpine

    Engelmann spruce and subalpine fir, dense and dark. Snow lies in the shaded draws into July, then the meadows flower all at once.

  3. 8,000 – 9,500 ft

    Montane

    Ponderosa on the dry south-facing slopes, aspen and blue spruce in the wet draws. The last elevation where a hard winter is survivable above ground.

Each entry's accent colour is sampled from its own photograph. The alpine reads grey because it is grey — colour only arrives further down the slope.

01

AnimalAlpine

American Pika

Ochotona princeps

A rabbit relative the size of a fist, living in the loose rock above treeline. It never hibernates. Instead it spends the short summer cutting wildflowers and grasses and curing them in haypiles under the talus — a winter pantry, stacked and turned to keep it from rotting.

Its insulation is so good that heat, not cold, is the danger. A few hours much above 78°F can kill it, which is why the pika is one of the first animals ecologists check when they want to know how fast the high country is warming.

Elevation
10,000 – 13,500 ft
Winter strategy
Haypile, no hibernation
Listen for
A single sharp squeak
02

AnimalAlpine

Yellow-bellied Marmot

Marmota flaviventris

The largest ground squirrel in the range, and the pika's neighbour in the same boulder fields — though the marmot takes the opposite bet. It eats relentlessly through summer, then sleeps through as much as eight months of the year, surfacing in spring having burned off close to half its body weight.

Marmots live in colonies and post sentries. The alarm is a rising whistle that carries across a basin, which is how they earned the name whistle pig from Colorado miners.

Elevation
6,500 – 13,000 ft
Hibernation
Up to eight months
Listen for
A rising two-note whistle

Rocky Mountain Bighorn Sheep

Ovis canadensis canadensis

Colorado's state animal, and the reason the state protects more bighorn habitat than any other. A mature ram's horns can weigh thirty pounds — more than every bone in his skeleton put together. He carries them up terrain that would stop a climber.

The trick is underfoot: a split hoof with a hard rim and a soft, concave centre that grips rock like a climbing shoe. During the November rut, rams charge each other at around twenty miles an hour, and the collision can be heard a mile off.

6,000 – 14,000 ft

1961
Ram horns
Up to 30 lb

Colorado Blue Columbine

Aquilegia coerulea

The state flower since 1899, and the one plant most Coloradans can name on sight. The blue is in the sepals; the white cups behind them are the petals, and each one trails a long hollow spur backwards like a comet's tail.

Those spurs are a lock. Nectar sits at the far end, out of reach of most insects, so the flower is pollinated mainly by hawkmoths and a few long-tongued bees. State law makes it illegal to uproot one on public land — pick the bloom if you must, but leave the plant.

Elevation
7,000 – 12,000 ft
State flower since
1899
Pollinated by
Hawkmoths, long-tongued bees

PlantSubalpine

Indian Paintbrush

Castilleja spp.

The scarlet is a bluff. What looks like petals are bracts — modified leaves flushed red to pull in hummingbirds. The real flowers are the thin green tubes hiding among them, easy to miss entirely.

Paintbrush is also a part-time thief. Its roots seek out neighbouring plants, puncture them, and siphon off water and minerals, which is why it resists transplanting and turns up reliably beside sagebrush and bunchgrass rather than alone.

Elevation
6,000 – 11,500 ft
Strategy
Hemiparasitic on neighbours
Colour comes from
Bracts, not petals

AnimalSubalpine

Rocky Mountain Elk

Cervus canadensis nelsoni

Colorado holds the largest elk herd on earth, somewhere near a quarter of a million animals — a fact that reads oddly against the history. By 1910 market hunting had driven them almost out of the state, and the herds you see today descend largely from animals trucked in from Wyoming and released.

A bull regrows his antlers from bare skull every spring, at up to an inch a day. In late September he bugles: a rising whistle that breaks into a grunt, thin and carrying, audible across a whole valley at dusk.

Elevation
6,000 – 11,000 ft
Antler growth
Up to 1 in per day
Rut
September – October

PlantMontane

Fireweed

Chamaenerion angustifolium

Named for what it does after a burn. Fireweed is one of the first plants back onto scorched ground and avalanche debris, arriving on silky wind-borne seeds — tens of thousands per plant — and holding the slope together while slower species catch up.

The spike opens from the bottom upward, which makes it a usable calendar. When the last flowers at the tip are open, high-country summer is effectively over.

Elevation
5,000 – 11,000 ft
Role
Pioneer after fire
Reads as
A calendar, bottom to top
08

PlantMontane

Quaking Aspen

Populus tremuloides

The sound comes first. Each leaf hangs from a flattened stem, so it pivots in air too slight to feel — a grove is audible before it is visible. It is the most widespread tree in North America and the one that turns whole Colorado hillsides yellow in the last week of September.

A grove is usually not a crowd of trees but one organism: a single root system sending up thousands of genetically identical stems. The bark is photosynthetic too, faintly green under the white powder, so an aspen keeps making sugar after its leaves are gone.

Elevation
6,500 – 10,000 ft
Structure
Clonal — one root, many stems
Bark
Photosynthetic year-round

04 — Field notes

The bands are
moving up.

A life zone is not a line on the mountain, it is a temperature the mountain happens to hold at that height. As the high country warms, those temperatures climb the slope, and everything keyed to them tries to climb with them.

For most species that means moving uphill. For the ones already at the top it means running out of mountain. American pika have disappeared from a number of lower talus fields in the Great Basin over the past century, and researchers watch Colorado's colonies for the same pattern. The pika cannot go higher than the summit.

What helps is unglamorous: staying on trail so tundra that takes decades to heal is not trampled in an afternoon, keeping distance during the elk rut and lambing season, and leaving the columbine in the ground.
